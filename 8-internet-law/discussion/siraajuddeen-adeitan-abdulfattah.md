## Question 1

I think the easiest way to see how law builds trust online is through the GDPR. People will only shop or share data online if they believe someone is watching out for them, and Solomons et al. (2022) make the point that clear rules lower the risk consumers feel in digital markets. The GDPR does this by requiring companies that handle EU residents’ data to explain what they collect, collect only what they need, and respect rights like access and erasure. The penalties are serious, reaching 4% of global turnover (European Parliament & Council, 2016). It isn’t just on paper either: in 2019, France’s regulator fined Google €50 million over unclear consent for personalized ads (CNIL, 2019).

## Question 2

Intellectual property law keeps online competition honest by making sure people can’t simply profit from someone else’s work (LawShelf, n.d.). The Napster case is a good illustration. In A&M Records v. Napster (2001), the court ruled that Napster was liable for helping users swap copyrighted music without permission. Beyond the verdict, it showed that digital content has real value, and it pushed the industry toward licensed services. That fits Ronteau et al.’s (2023) argument that a digital business model only works if the value it creates can actually be captured.

## Question 3

One challenge I find especially concerning is manipulation through design. The FTC alleged that Amazon used “dark patterns” that signed people up for Prime easily but made cancelling confusing (FTC, 2023). When a design exploits human habits and biases, customers aren’t really consenting, and their autonomy suffers. Businesses should respond by being upfront, making opt-out as simple as opt-in, auditing their algorithms for ethical risks, and building privacy into products from the start. Ronteau et al. (2023) suggest long-term value depends on trust, so meeting the legal minimum shouldn’t be the goal.
