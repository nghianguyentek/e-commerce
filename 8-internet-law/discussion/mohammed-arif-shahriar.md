Legal frameworks establish institutional trust in digital markets by constraining predatory practices (Ronteau et al., 2023). For example, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) polices deceptive commercial conduct and protects digital consumers. It enforces guidelines on influencer marketing, algorithmic transparency, and data disclosures, ensuring businesses cannot deploy deceptive endorsements or unverified claims that exploit information asymmetries online (Solomons et al., 2022).

Intellectual property (IP) protections safeguard original brand equity and deter bad-faith market free-riding (LawShelf, n.d.). In e-commerce, this integrity is demonstrated by Nike v. StockX, where Nike sued the resale platform for trademark infringement over its issuance of unauthorized Vault NFTs depicting Nike footwear. This dispute highlighted that digital representations in web3 and virtual commerce cannot dilute or misappropriate registered brand marks without explicit authorization, reinforcing ethical competitive boundaries (LawShelf, n.d.; Ronteau et al., 2023).

A critical ethical challenge in algorithmic commerce is behavioral hyper-nudging and addictive engagement loops (Ronteau et al., 2023). Platforms deploy predictive artificial intelligence and micro-targeted tracking to monetize consumer attention, frequently manipulating emotional vulnerabilities through opaque "dark patterns" (Solomons et al., 2022). To operate responsibly, enterprises must transition to "privacy by design," maintain transparent algorithmic governance, and give consumers accessible opt-outs from predictive behavioral profiling. Aligning data capture with genuine consumer utility rather than covert manipulation preserves long-term platform credibility and averts severe regulatory backlash (Ronteau et al., 2023).
