## 1. How Amazon Creates and Delivers Value Differently from Traditional Businesses

Traditional retail businesses generally create value through a linear model: they source products, add margin, and sell
to customers through physical infrastructure that limits reach, inventory turnover, and personalization. Amazon, by
contrast, illustrates what Ronteau, Muzellec, Saxena, and Trabucchi (2023) describe as the shift in digital business
models toward mechanisms of value creation and capture that are structurally different from industrial-era firms.

### Value creation differences:

- Platform and ecosystem logic: Amazon does not simply sell goods; it operates a multi-sided platform connecting
  third-party sellers, logistics partners, content creators (via Prime Video, Kindle Direct Publishing), and cloud
  customers (AWS). This mirrors the digital business model logic in Ronteau et al. (2023), where value is generated
  through orchestrating an ecosystem rather than through a single linear supply chain.
- Data-driven personalization: Every customer interaction (browsing, purchase history, reviews) feeds into
  recommendation algorithms, allowing Amazon to tailor offers at an individual level — something a traditional retailer
  with a fixed shelf and generic catalog cannot replicate.
- Scalability without proportional cost: Digital infrastructure allows Amazon to serve millions of customers
  simultaneously with marginal costs that fall as volume rises, unlike a physical store constrained by shelf space and
  staff hours.

### Value delivery differences:

- Speed and convenience replace the traditional value delivery of in-store service — same-day/next-day delivery,
  one-click purchasing, and subscription-based access (Prime) redefine the customer experience around frictionless,
  always-on availability.
- Continuous engagement rather than transactional encounters: traditional businesses typically deliver value at the
  point of sale, whereas Amazon maintains ongoing relationships through subscriptions, personalized marketing, and
  post-purchase services, which aligns with the digital marketing principle that value delivery in e-commerce is
  relational and continuous rather than episodic (Solomons, Kliphuis & Wadley, 2022).

### Value capture differences:

- Amazon captures value not only through direct product margins but through multiple complementary revenue streams —
  subscription fees (Prime), advertising revenue from sellers, cloud computing (AWS), and marketplace commissions. This
  diversified capture mechanism is a hallmark of digital business models identified by Ronteau et al. (2023), where
  monetization is decoupled from the core product transaction itself.

## 2. Why Some E-commerce Business Models Fail While Others Thrive

### Key reasons for failure:

- Poor unit economics and unsustainable customer acquisition costs: Many e-commerce ventures spend heavily to acquire
  customers without a viable path to profitability per transaction.

  Example: Webvan, an online grocery delivery company from the early dot-com era, invested enormously in warehouses and
  logistics infrastructure but could not generate enough order volume or margin to cover its fixed costs, leading to its
  collapse in 2001. Its failure reflects what Ronteau et al. (2023) frame as a mismatch between the value creation
  architecture (heavy physical infrastructure) and a value capture model that assumed unrealistic demand growth.

- Lack of differentiated value proposition or genuine customer understanding: Digital marketing frameworks emphasize
  that businesses must deeply understand customer needs and communicate a distinct value proposition (Solomons,
  Kliphuis & Wadley, 2022); companies that merely replicate existing offerings online without adapting to digital
  consumer behavior often fail to build loyalty.

  Example: Pets.com became a symbol of dot-com failure because it pursued aggressive branding and marketing spend
  without a sustainable logistics model or clear differentiation from competitors, resulting in unsustainable losses and
  closure in 2000.

### Key reasons for success:

- Network effects and ecosystem orchestration: Businesses that succeed in the digital economy often build platforms
  where value increases as more participants join — buyers attract sellers, sellers attract more buyers, and data
  improves matching and personalization over time. This is central to the digital business model logic described by
  Ronteau et al. (2023).

  Example: Amazon's marketplace grew stronger as more third-party sellers joined, which increased product variety,
  attracted more buyers, and reinforced Amazon's data advantage — a self-reinforcing loop traditional retailers cannot
  easily replicate.

- Alignment of value proposition with customer-centric digital marketing strategy: Thriving e-commerce firms
  continuously adapt their offerings based on customer data and feedback loops, consistent with the customer-centric
  marketing principles outlined in Solomons, Kliphuis, and Wadley (2022).

  Example: HubSpot succeeded by identifying a specific customer pain point (fragmented marketing and sales tools for
  small-to-medium businesses) and building an integrated, easy-to-adopt platform, continuously iterating based on user
  feedback and inbound marketing principles — allowing it to scale efficiently while maintaining strong customer
  retention.
