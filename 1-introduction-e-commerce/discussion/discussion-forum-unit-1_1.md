## How the Internet and emerging digital technologies have changed business–customer interaction

The shift from analogue to networked commerce has fundamentally altered where and how value is created and exchanged. Solomons, Kliphuis and Wadley (2022) frame their most recent edition against the backdrop of rapid societal shifts — most notably the pandemic-driven surge in digital communication and connection — which has reshaped everyday marketing practice. This has pushed customer interaction almost entirely into digital channels: e-commerce, digital advertising, social media platforms, and content marketing are now core touchpoints rather than supplementary ones (Solomons et al., 2022).

Several concrete shifts stand out:

- **From one-way broadcast to two-way, real-time dialogue.** Traditional media (TV, print, radio) pushed a single message to a passive audience. Social media and mobile apps let customers respond, review, complain, and co-create in real time, forcing businesses to monitor and manage reputation continuously rather than periodically.
- **From mass marketing to personalisation at scale.** AI-driven data analytics allow firms to track behaviour (browsing, purchase history, location) and tailor offers, content, and pricing to individuals or micro-segments — something impossible with pre-digital tools.
- **From product-centred to platform- and ecosystem-centred value creation.** Ronteau, Muzellec, Saxena and Trabucchi (2023) frame digital business models around new mechanisms of value creation and capture — for example, platforms that connect multiple customer groups (buyers, sellers, advertisers) and monetise the data and interactions generated between them, rather than simply selling a discrete product.
- **From episodic transactions to continuous relationships.** Mobile apps and always-on connectivity mean the “customer journey” no longer ends at purchase; businesses now manage ongoing engagement through push notifications, loyalty apps, chatbots, and AI-based customer service that operate 24/7.
- **Lower barriers to entry and disintermediation.** Digital infrastructure has allowed new entrants to bypass traditional intermediaries (retailers, agencies, distributors), building direct-to-consumer relationships and new value-capture mechanisms such as subscription, freemium, or data-monetisation models (Ronteau et al., 2023).

In short, digital technologies have moved the locus of customer interaction from a fixed point of sale to a continuous, data-rich, multi-channel relationship, and they have expanded what counts as a “product” to include the surrounding digital experience and ecosystem (Ronteau et al., 2023; Solomons et al., 2022).

## 2. The evolution from traditional marketing to inbound/digital strategies

Traditional marketing relied heavily on outbound, interruption-based tactics — TV and radio spots, print ads, cold calling, direct mail — that pushed messages to broad audiences regardless of interest or intent. Solomons et al. (2022) structure their text around this shift, covering strategy, customer behaviour and market research alongside newer digital-specific competencies such as search, social media and content marketing, reflecting how marketing practice itself has broadened.

Key elements of this evolution include:

- **From interruption to attraction (inbound marketing).** Rather than pushing ads at people, businesses now create valuable content — blog posts, videos, guides, social posts — designed to be found by customers who are already searching or browsing, and to pull them toward the brand. Search engine optimisation (SEO) and content marketing are central tools here, replacing the “spray and pray” logic of mass advertising (Solomons et al., 2022).
- **From generic messaging to targeted, data-driven campaigns.** Digital advertising platforms allow granular targeting by demographics, interests, and behaviour, and campaign performance can be tracked through analytics and KPIs in ways traditional media never allowed — enabling continuous optimisation rather than a one-off “launch and hope” campaign.
- **From single-channel to integrated multi-channel presence.** Modern marketing spans search, social media, email, and owned web/app properties simultaneously, with a consistent brand experience across each — a departure from the single dominant channel (e.g., TV) that characterised traditional campaigns.
- **From acquisition-only to retention and lifetime value.** Traditional marketing was heavily weighted toward customer acquisition. Digital tools (email automation, retargeting, loyalty apps, personalised recommendations) now make retention and repeat engagement just as central, since digital businesses often capture value through recurring relationships (subscriptions, platform usage, data) rather than single transactions (Ronteau et al., 2023).
- **From marketing as a function to marketing as embedded in the business model.** Ronteau et al. (2023) argue that in digital business models, value creation and value capture are tightly interwoven with how customers are attracted, engaged, and monetised — meaning marketing is no longer a separate department bolted onto a fixed product, but part of the core mechanism by which the business generates revenue (e.g., a platform's growth strategy is itself a marketing and business-model decision).

Together, these shifts show marketing moving from a broadcast, transaction-focused discipline to an always-on, relationship- and data-driven discipline that is deeply integrated with the broader digital business model of the firm (Ronteau et al., 2023; Solomons et al., 2022).
